## Abstract

Shape matching is widely used for real-time deformable bodies because it is fast and stays stable at large time steps. It already handles large *elastic* deformation and ductile fracture, but previous work only supported modest *plastic* deformation. This paper simulates solids that undergo large plastic flow and topology change with shape matching inside the position based dynamics (PBD) framework, extending PBD (already used for rigid bodies, liquids, gases, cloth and moderately plastic solids). The new pieces are: **local particle re-sampling**, **cluster re-sampling**, and **skinning of an explicitly tracked surface mesh**.

*ACM CCS:* I.3.3 [Computer Graphics]: Animation / Physical Simulation.

## 1. Introduction

Shape matching represents a solid by particles held together by a compliant geometric constraint; it is stable and cheap enough for real time. Since it pulls particles toward a well defined goal configuration it naturally suits elastic or nearly rigid materials. Many real materials — taffy, dough, foams — additionally need *large* plastic deformation.

Prior plasticity extensions for shape matching ([MHT05], [Cho14], [JML 16]) modify the particles' reference configuration according to a heuristic constitutive law. That breaks down for large plastic flow: particles drift apart and leave regions badly under-sampled, and the original shape-matching groups can no longer express all deformation modes. Existing particle re-sampling schemes ([SB12], [APKG07], [ATT12], [JWJ 14]) do not directly carry over to re-sampling *shape-matching constraints*.

The paper therefore inserts and deletes shape-matching clusters locally, driven by simple deformation tests, which permits essentially unbounded deformation and works with any shape-matching simulator. It also tackles rendering: shape matching outputs per-cluster affine transforms that are usually fed to linear blend skinning ([BM82], [MTLT88]) of a visual mesh — fine for small deformation without topology change, but not for tearing and fracture. Here the visual mesh is tracked explicitly [CM15] and a new skinning method respects the changing simulation.

Main contributions:

1. A local re-sampling scheme for particles and shape-matching clusters.
2. An improved skinning method for the explicitly tracked surface mesh.

## 2. Related Work

**PBD and shape matching.** PBD [MHR06] formulates constrained dynamics directly on positions and has been extended to multi-resolution [Mül08], elastic rods [USS14], fluids [MM13], smoke and granular media [MMCK14]; [BMM15] surveys the area. Müller et al. [MHT05] introduced shape matching: the best-fit transformation of the current particle set defines goal positions toward which particles are pulled. Rivers and James [RJ07] accelerated it with fast summation on lattices; Steinemann et al. [SOG08] generalized that to adaptive lattices. Bargteil and Jones [BJ14] added strain limiting for stability with explicit integration. Choi [Cho14] defined a strain measure inside shape matching and used it for plasticity and ductile fracture. Jones et al. [JML 15] proposed weighted clusters for artistic control and later [JML 16] a plasticity/fracture model inspired by the FEM work of [BWHT07]. None of these address *very large* plastic deformation within shape matching.

**Other plasticity methods.** FEM: O'Brien et al. [OBH02] (ductile fracture), Bargteil et al. [BWHT07] (large plastic flow), Wojtan et al. [WT08] (thin features) and [WTGT09] (topology change). Eulerian: Goktekin et al. [GBO04] (viscoelastic solids), extended to multiple liquids [LSSF06]. MPM: Stomakhin et al. [SSC 13] (snow) and [SSJ 14] (phase change); Ram et al. [RGJ 15] (foams, sponges).

**Particle methods.** Particles suit large plastic deformation because thin features, topology change and volume conservation are easy. Müller et al. [MKN 04] computed a deformation gradient from particles via moving least squares (MLS) for elastic/plastic solids; [PKA 05] extended this to ductile fracture; Keiser et al. [KAG 05] unified solid-to-fluid transition. Clavet et al. [CBP05] used springs that are inserted/removed dynamically for viscoelastic fluids. SPH has been extended to plastic solids: Solenthaler et al. [SSP07] (unified fluids/solids/rigids); Peer et al. [PICT15] (implicit viscosity with a single projection). Hieber and Koumoutsakos [HK08] fit the deformation gradient by least squares each step without a rest state; Gerszewski et al. [GBB09] combined local best-fit affine maps (no rest state, so only limited elasticity); Jones et al. [JWJ 14] add an embedded space between rest and world space. A common weakness of particle methods is that the deformation gradient is not unique in sparse regions, causing instability; storing per-particle orientation is a common cure ([MKB 10], [MC11], [FGBP11]).

**Particle re-sampling.** Schechter and Bridson [SB12] used Fast Poisson Disk Sampling [Bri07] to sample fluid and ghost particles evenly; Ando et al. [ATT12] re-sample FLIP [ZB05] particles in thin sheets; Chentanez et al. [CM15] use Poisson disk sampling when converting grid liquid to particles; Adams et al. [APKG07] split/merge SPH particles adaptively; Jones et al. [JWJ 14] split/merge for stability of a point-based solid. This paper's particle re-sampling borrows the Fast Poisson Disk Sampling idea.

**Skinning.** Standard options are linear blend skinning [BM82], [MTLT88], multi-weight enveloping [WP02], animation space [MMG06] and dual quaternion skinning [KCvO08]; see the course notes of Jacobson et al. [JDKL14].

## 3. The Method

An object is a set of particles plus an explicit triangle surface mesh. Each time step has three stages: (1) advance particle state with elastic and plastic deformation; (2) advect the explicit surface with the particles by skinning and resolve topology changes; (3) re-sample particles and update clusters.

### 3.1 Simulating Elastic and Plastic Deformation

Clustered shape matching [MHT05] is used as a constraint inside a parallel PBD solver [MMCK14]; plasticity follows Choi [Cho14] (the model of Jones et al. [JML 16] would also work).

A solid is discretized into particles $i \in P$ with mass $m_i$, position $\mathbf{x}_i$ and velocity $\mathbf{v}_i$. One step of size $\Delta t$:

$$\mathbf{v}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{v}_i + \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}_{ext}(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad \mathbf{x}^*_i \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_i + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_i .$$

The solver then runs several projection iterations on the predicted positions. In each iteration every positional constraint is projected in parallel; the corrections from all constraints touching particle $i$ are summed into $\Delta\mathbf{x}_i$ and applied as an average,

$$\mathbf{x}^*_i \leftarrow \mathbf{x}^*_i + \Delta\mathbf{x}_i / n_i ,$$

where $n_i$ is the number of constraints affecting particle $i$. After the iterations,

$$\mathbf{v}_i \leftarrow \frac{1}{\Delta t}\,(\mathbf{x}^*_i - \mathbf{x}_i), \qquad \mathbf{x}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{x}^*_i .$$

Contact handling and further details follow [MMCK14].

**Clustered shape matching with a plastic matrix.** Clusters are overlapping particle subsets. For a cluster $C$, let $F_P$ be its plastic deformation matrix (initialized to $I$), $\mathbf{q}_i$ the local rest position of particle $i$ relative to the cluster's center of mass, and $\mathbf{c}$ the current center of mass. With $\mathbf{p}_i$ the current (predicted) particle positions:

$$A_{pq} = \Big(\sum_{i\in C} m_i\,(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{c})\,\mathbf{q}_i^{T}\Big) F_P^{T} \tag{1}$$

$$A_{qq} = F_P \Big(\sum_{i\in C} m_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^{T}\Big) F_P^{T} \tag{2}$$

A polar decomposition $A_{pq}A_{qq}^{-1} = R\,S_{pqqq}$ yields the rotation $R$ from the (plastically deformed) rest pose to the current pose. Goals and corrections are

$$\mathbf{g}_i = R\,F_P\,\mathbf{q}_i + \mathbf{c}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{x}_i \leftarrow \Delta\mathbf{x}_i + K\,(\mathbf{g}_i - \mathbf{x}_i),$$

with cluster stiffness $0 \le K \le 1$. Note that the goal is the *rigidly* rotated plastic rest shape — no linear/stretch term is blended in.

**Plastic update (Choi [Cho14]).** Diagonalize $A_{qq}$:

$$A_{qq} = V_{qq}\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda^{qq}_1, \lambda^{qq}_2, \lambda^{qq}_3)\,V_{qq}^{T} \tag{3}$$

$$S_{pq} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(R^{T}A_{pq} + A_{pq}^{T}R\big) \tag{4}$$

**[reconstructed]** — the body of Eq. (4) was lost in extraction; this form is the one consistent with Eq. (5): the least-squares symmetric stretch $S$ for a fixed $R$ satisfies $S A_{qq} + A_{qq} S = R^{T}A_{pq} + A_{pq}^{T}R$, whose solution in the eigenbasis of $A_{qq}$ is exactly Eq. (5).

The optimal stretch is

$$S = V_{qq}\Big[\big(V_{qq}^{T} S_{pq} V_{qq}\big) \circ \bar{\Lambda}^{qq}\Big] V_{qq}^{T}, \qquad \bar{\Lambda}^{qq}_{ij} = \frac{2}{\lambda^{qq}_i + \lambda^{qq}_j}, \tag{5}$$

where $\circ$ is the Hadamard (element-wise) product. Next decompose $S = V\Lambda V^{T}$ with $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)$ and use the logarithmic strain

$$\epsilon_l = V \log(\Lambda)\, V^{T}, \qquad \|\epsilon_l\| = \max_i \lvert\log\lambda_i\rvert .$$

With a multiplicative plasticity model as in [ITF04], the plastic matrix is updated as

$$F_P \leftarrow V\big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{-1/3}\,\Lambda\big)^{\gamma}\,V^{T} F_P, \qquad \gamma = \min\!\Big(\frac{\nu\,(\|\epsilon_l\| - \epsilon_Y)}{\|\epsilon_l\|},\ 1\Big), \tag{6}$$

where $\lvert\Lambda\rvert = \det\Lambda$ (so the increment is volume preserving), $\gamma$ is the fraction of the elastic deformation transferred to the plastic part, $\nu$ is the plastic flow rate and $\epsilon_Y$ the plastic yield. (Flow only occurs when $\|\epsilon_l\| > \epsilon_Y$; $\gamma \le 0$ means no update — implied by the meaning of a yield threshold, not spelled out in the text.)

*Work hardening* (optional): $\epsilon_Y \leftarrow \epsilon_Y + \kappa\,\|\epsilon_l\|\,\Delta t$, where $\kappa$ controls the amount of hardening.

*Stability clamp:* under violent interaction, each diagonal entry of $\big(\lvert\Lambda\rvert^{-1/3}\Lambda\big)^{\gamma}$ is clamped to $[0.8,\ 1.2]$, which roughly limits the change of $F_P$ to 20% per time step.

### 3.2 Visual Mesh Skinning

The visual mesh is skinned to the moving **particles** (not to the clusters) in two steps.

1. **Per-particle orientation.** Each cluster's rotation $R$ is converted to a quaternion; a particle's orientation $Q_i$ is the normalized average of the quaternions of all clusters that contain it — the best-fit rotation under a geodesic distance [Gra01].
2. **Vertex update.** Every visual vertex stores up to four particle indices $l_a$, local offsets $\mathbf{z}_a$ and weights $w_a$ ($1 \le a \le 4$). Its position is

$$\mathbf{y} = \sum_{a=1}^{4} w_a\big(Q^{M}_{l_a}\,\mathbf{z}_a + \mathbf{x}_{l_a}\big),$$

where $Q^{M}_{l_a}$ is the rotation matrix of quaternion $Q_{l_a}$.

Rationale: particles carry positional detail that clusters cannot represent, so the mesh follows particles; particles have no orientation of their own, so it is interpolated from clusters. After the new vertex positions are computed, topology changes are resolved and triangle quality is maintained with the method of [CMMK15]. In that step, visual vertices with no particle within $2r$ are deleted, which may split the surface.

### 3.3 Re-Sampling

Re-sampling runs as a sequence of sub-steps: remove invalid particles and seed new ones in under-sampled parts of the visual mesh; update existing clusters, delete invalid clusters and add new clusters where needed; finally adjust the visual mesh skinning data.

Summary of the per-step pipeline (condensed from the prose of §3.3; the paper gives no listing):

```text
    step(dt):
      PBD predict; project clustered shape-matching constraints (Eqs. 1-2, goal g = R Fp q + c)
      per cluster: plastic update of Fp (Eqs. 3-6, hardening, [0.8,1.2] clamp)
      skin visual mesh to particles (§3.2); explicit surface tracking / topology fix [CMMK15]
      resample:
        component IDs: mesh -> particles -> BFS inward; delete clusters spanning >1 ID   (§3.3.7)
        delete particles: too close (< h r to a lower-index neighbour) or
                          outside inflated mesh and unreferenced by skinning              (§3.3.1)
        seed particles: T tries per particle of clusters with ||Fp||_F > 1.8,
                        shell [r, 2r], > r from all particles, inside inflated mesh      (§3.3.2)
        update clusters: drop deleted members, add new particles within r^C,
                         q = W (x - c), W = Fp^-1 Aqq Apq^-1, recentre q                  (§3.3.3)
        delete clusters: < 0.5x or > 2x initial count, ||Fp||_F > 2.0,
                         or every member in > M_maxother clusters                         (§3.3.4)
        add clusters: Poisson-like centre pick among particles in < M_min clusters,
                      Fp = I, q = W_new (x - c) with W_new interpolated from old clusters (§3.3.5)
        fix skinning indices/weights/offsets of affected vertices; optional volume fix    (§3.3.6)
```

#### 3.3.1 Remove Invalid Particles

Over time some particles crowd too close to others or end up far outside the visual mesh, so they no longer contribute. A particle is flagged for deletion if either holds:

- (a) its index is smaller than its closest neighbor's index **and** the distance to that neighbor is below $h\,r$ ($r$ = particle radius, $h$ a small constant; $h = 0.1$ in all examples);
- (b) it lies outside the *inflated* visual mesh **and** no visual vertex references it for skinning. The inflated mesh has the visual mesh's topology with each vertex pushed out along its normal by $0.25\,r$.

Inside/outside tests use ray casting.

#### 3.3.2 Seed New Particles

Plastic flow can pull particles apart and leave holes. For every particle belonging to a *significantly deformed* cluster — one whose plastic matrix has Frobenius norm $\|F_P\|_F > 1.8$ — the method tries $T = 5$ candidate positions, drawn uniformly from the shell between concentric spheres of radius $r$ and $2r$ around it [Bri07]. A candidate becomes a new particle (copying the source particle's physical quantities) if it is more than $r$ from its nearest particle, inside the inflated visual mesh, and not inside other scene geometry.

#### 3.3.3 Update Clusters

After particle deletion/insertion, each cluster $C$ drops deleted members and adopts every new particle with $\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c}\| < r^C$, where $\mathbf{c}$ is the cluster's center of mass and $r^C$ its undeformed radius. An adopted particle gets the local position

$$\mathbf{q}_i = W(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c}), \qquad W = F_P^{-1} A_{qq} A_{pq}^{-1},$$

i.e. $W$ maps world space back into the cluster's (pre-plastic) local space. If membership changed, the local positions are re-centered so their mass-weighted mean is the origin:

$$\bar{\mathbf{c}} = \frac{\sum_{i\in C} m_i\,\mathbf{q}_i}{\sum_{i\in C} m_i}, \qquad \mathbf{q}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{q}_i - \bar{\mathbf{c}} .$$

#### 3.3.4 Remove Invalid Clusters

Clusters that gained or lost many particles, deformed plastically a lot, or overlap heavily with others no longer represent the deformation field well. They are simply deleted; deletion triggers creation of replacement clusters (§3.3.5), which is how cluster re-sampling happens. A cluster is deleted if any of:

- it has fewer than half, or more than twice, its initial particle count;
- $\|F_P\|_F > 2.0$;
- every one of its particles is a member of more than $M_{maxother}$ other clusters.

#### 3.3.5 Add New Clusters

After deletions, some particles are covered by too few clusters, and new particles by none.

1. Build $L_{cand}$: particles referenced by fewer than $M_{min}$ clusters.
2. Repeatedly pick a random unmarked particle of $L_{cand}$, append it to $L_{cen}$, and mark all $L_{cand}$ particles within $r^G$ of it ($r^G < r^C$ is the cluster-creation spacing), until all are marked.
3. Create one cluster per particle in $L_{cen}$, containing all particles within $r^C$.

A new cluster starts with $F_P = I$; its plastic history is instead *embedded* into the local positions, $\mathbf{q}_i = W_{new}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c})$, where $W_{new}$ is interpolated from the old clusters (including those deleted this step), following Bargteil et al. [BWHT07]:

$$U = W^{-1} = A_{pq}A_{qq}^{-1}F_P, \qquad G = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(U^{T}U - I\big)\ \text{for every old cluster}.$$

$G_{new}$ is a weighted average of the old clusters' $G$, with weights proportional to the overlap volume of the radius-$r^C$ spheres centered on the clusters. Then

$$2G_{new} + I = V_{new} D_{new} V_{new}^{T}, \qquad D_{new} = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda^{new}_1, \lambda^{new}_2, \lambda^{new}_3),$$

with $V_{new}$ a rotation; for stability each $\lambda^{new}_i$ is clamped to $[0.25,\ 4]$, and

$$W_{new} = V_{new}\,\mathrm{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{new}_1}}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{new}_2}}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{new}_3}}\Big)\,V_{new}^{T}.$$

The plastic yield $\epsilon_Y$ is interpolated with the same weights. Clusters are generated once per time step, so in principle a particle could need up to $M_{min}$ steps to reach $M_{min}$ clusters; in practice new particles sit near existing clusters and join them immediately.

#### 3.3.6 Update visual mesh and the skinning weights as needed

When particles are deleted or change cluster membership, the skinning data of visual vertices must be refreshed. First, particle orientations $Q_i$ are re-interpolated from clusters (old values are invalid after membership changes). Then, for each vertex whose indices $l_a$ point at deleted particles, $l_a$ is reset to the four closest particles with weights $w_a$ proportional to inverse distance, normalized to sum to 1. The local offsets are recomputed,

$$\mathbf{z}_a = \big(Q^{M}_{l_a}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}_{l_a}\big),$$

(**[reconstructed]**: the inverse/transposed rotation is implied by inverting the skinning formula of §3.2), but only when needed, i.e. when $l_a$ changes or $Q_{l_a}$ changes due to re-sampling.

*Volume conservation (optional, used for bunny, taffy and dough).* Similar to Thuerey et al. [TWGT10], visual vertices are moved along their normals by (target volume − current volume) / current surface area, separately per connected component of the visual mesh. One pass per time step suffices (iterating until the error is below a tolerance is possible). Moved vertices get their $\mathbf{z}_a$ recomputed; the movement is small enough that indices and weights need not be recomputed.

#### 3.3.7 Handling multiple connected components

Objects can split through tearing, user manipulation, etc. Unless particles separate very fast, the method so far would *prevent* splitting, because new clusters would bridge particles that belong to different pieces. Fix:

- Find connected components of the visual mesh; each vertex stores its component ID.
- Propagate IDs to particles through the skinning indices. A particle referenced by vertices of several components is duplicated (one copy per component, with a small positional offset). IDs then spread inward to particles far from the surface by breadth-first search.
- Any cluster whose members carry different IDs is marked for deletion. These steps run before invalid-particle removal.
- Particle removal only considers the nearest particle *of the same ID*; seeded particles inherit the source particle's ID; cluster update only adds same-ID particles; cluster creation only groups same-ID particles and interpolates only from same-ID old clusters; skinning indices only use same-ID particles.
- When mesh components merge they share an ID again, so particles from previously separate parts can form clusters.
- A user cut deletes all triangles crossing the cut plane, splitting the mesh into components, and marks the involved vertices as excluded from the pairing score throughout the pairing step of [CMMK15].

## 4. Results and Discussion

The algorithm was implemented on the GPU; the unoptimized prototype runs at real-time or interactive rates on one NVIDIA GTX TitanX for all examples, including simulation, surface tracking, re-sampling and rendering. All examples use $r = 0.1$, $r^G = 3r$ and $r^C = 4r$; objects measure between $20r$ and $100r$.

Examples (videos accompany the paper): an armadillo shot at several places (Fig. 1); an armadillo dropped on the ground with different flow rates and yield thresholds (Fig. 2) and different hardening coefficients (Fig. 3); a bunny pulled and shot (Fig. 4); a cow caught between moving bars (Fig. 5); a taffy-pulling machine (Fig. 6); dough squeezed, rolled, folded and split (Fig. 7); dough manipulated by hands through a hand-tracking device (Fig. 8). These show that re-sampling survives very large plastic flow.

Without re-sampling the simulation either **becomes unstable** (if the plastic matrix is not clamped) or **cannot reach large deformation** (if it is clamped), and it cannot change topology (shown in a video).

Timings are in Table 1. Surface topology handling and triangle-quality maintenance usually dominate, since the surface mesh is fine relative to the particle spacing. Within re-sampling, particle deletion and addition are the expensive parts because of ray-cast inside/outside tests whose cost grows with surface resolution; ray casting alone is about 60% of re-sampling time. Frame rates remain real-time or interactive. The share of particles considered for seeding ranges from 15% on average (bunny) to 80% (cow cutting), depending on how much plastic deformation occurs. Volume conservation is enabled for the bunny, taffy and dough.

## 5. Conclusion and Discussion

This is presented as the first shape-matching solid simulator that handles very large plastic deformation, achieved by re-sampling particles and clusters. Only the shape-matching constraints are affected, so the solid still interacts with liquids, gases, rigid bodies and other particle types of the unified framework [MMCK14].

Total mass is not explicitly conserved; the authors argue that preserving the *visual mesh volume* is enough for most uses, and since particle distributions stay good, mass is roughly conserved anyway. Separate objects get separate component IDs; running topology change and ray casting per object keeps them from merging. Cluster splitting due to fracture is not handled, but the methods of Choi [Cho14] or Jones et al. [JML 16] could be added. The approach should transfer to pure shape-matching simulators ([BJ14], [JML 15], [JML 16]), not only PBD.

Ray-cast inside/outside testing is the main re-sampling bottleneck; it need not be exact, so an approximate test could speed things up considerably. Clusters may still contain particles of the same component that are within $r^C$ but belong to parts of the surface that folded onto each other, so a single component folding over itself within $r^C$ can merge. This was not a problem in the examples (such particles are usually already approaching each other and the visual mesh merges too); using geodesic distance across overlapping particles instead of Euclidean distance to decide cluster membership is left as future work.
