Experienced derby drivers generally try to make **rear-end hits** their default because the front of the car houses the engine, radiator, and steering components that end a run if they fail[2][4][13]. The basic strategy is to stay mobile, strike hard enough to disable others, and avoid taking damage to your own front end until you absolutely have to[2][4][9].

## Core techniques and strategy

- **Backing up and hitting with the rear**
  - Drivers often use the rear of the car to attack so they can protect the radiator and engine in the front[2][4][13].
  - One driver interview said the rear is used “as much as possible” because front hits risk “killing your own car,” and the preferred kill shots are the opponent’s radiator, axles, or tires[2].

- **Aiming for vulnerable spots**
  - The best targets are usually the **front corners, front wheels, axles, and radiator area** because those areas can disable steering, puncture tires, or overheat the car[2][4][9].
  - Hitting the front wheels or axle can bend suspension parts or blow a tire, while radiator damage can end the car by overheating[2][4].

- **Sideswipes and glancing hits**
  - If a clean square hit is not available, drivers will often use glancing hits or sideswipes while passing to keep momentum and avoid exposing their own front end[4][9].
  - The advantage is that the car stays moving and can still deliver damage without stopping dead in front of an opponent[4][9].

- **Using the arena walls**
  - Walls are used to pin opponents, trap them, or deny them space to accelerate[9].
  - Drivers also try not to get pinned themselves, because being trapped against a wall makes the car an easy target for repeated hits[4][9].

- **J-turn / e-brake swing**
  - Drivers use quick reverse-to-forward transitions, often with the parking brake or e-brake, to swing the car around and line up a rear hit quickly[2][6].
  - This is part of why reverse-driving skill matters so much; one driver described it as the “prescribed way” to protect the motor while going after someone else[6].

- **When to fight early vs sandbag**
  - Early in a heat, many drivers pick aggressive targets only if it helps remove a threat quickly; otherwise they may stay back, let others damage each other, and conserve the car[3][4][9].
  - That behavior is often called **sandbagging**, and some rules require a car to keep making contact regularly or face disqualification[1][3][7].

- **Finishing off weakened cars**
  - Drivers commonly switch focus to **smoking, overheating, or visibly weakened cars** because those are closest to being eliminated[2][9].
  - The usual goal is to finish them with repeated rear hits, push them into bad angles, or target damaged steering and wheel components until they stop moving[2][4].

## How much time drivers spend going backwards

There is no universal percentage, but experienced accounts repeatedly describe **most serious hitting being done in reverse or with the rear end**, because that preserves the drivetrain and radiator[2][13]. In practice, drivers may still move forward to reposition, but the “big hits” are often made while backing up[13]. One interview also says drivers “really have to be good at driving in reverse” and will increasingly chase people down backward as the heat goes on[2].

## What makes a winner

- The winner is usually the **last car still able to move under its own power**[1][5][6].
- A good winner keeps the car mobile, avoids front-end damage, manages heat, and keeps making legal contact so they are not counted out for inactivity[1][3][4][7].
- Strong derby drivers combine offense and survival: they protect the radiator, choose collisions carefully, keep the car moving, and wait for the right moment to finish off damaged opponents[2][4][9].

## Concrete rules and numbers

- Some events require a car to **hit another vehicle every 2 minutes** or be judged sandbagging[1].
- Other rules use a **60-second clock** or similar warning system against passive driving[5].
- In some rule sets, a driver who is suspected of sandbagging can be disqualified, and some events explicitly require forward motion before backing up[3][7].
