A passenger car hitting a palm tree off-centre at 30–60 km/h will usually experience a strong deceleration plus a turn/yaw away from the impact side; the tree is more likely to absorb the hit than the car is to “bounce through” it cleanly[1][4][8]. Palm trunks are generally treated as rigid roadside hazards, and real crash data notes that palm trees often behave as unforgiving objects rather than yielding supports[5][13][14].

## What happens to the tree
- Palm trunks usually do **not** behave like a flexible roadside post; they are typically stiff, fibrous, and hazardous fixed objects[5][13][14].
- In documented roadside work, palm trees are specifically treated as crash hazards, and impact studies note that many tree crashes involved palm trees with substantial trunk width[5][10].
- Whether a palm trunk breaks or the tree uproots depends on size, soil, root structure, and hit height, but roadside guidance still classifies them as fixed-object hazards rather than “energy-absorbing” obstacles[2][13][15].
- In at least one reported high-energy palm impact test, the **top section of the palm snapped**, showing that trunk failure can occur under sufficient load, but that does not imply the tree behaves like a frangible pole[7].

## Compared with a steel pole or hardwood tree
- A **steel pole** is the closest thing to a very stiff narrow object; off-centre impacts against poles commonly produce vehicle rotation/yaw and do not rely on the pole yielding much[1][8].
- Roadside guidance treats trees and poles together as hazardous fixed objects, but trees are often thicker and less forgiving than slender breakaway-style objects[13].
- A **hardwood tree** is generally also a rigid fixed object; severe injury risk rises quickly even at moderate urban speeds, and a six-inch tree trunk at about 40 mph has been described as typically producing fatal outcomes[14].
- In contrast, a palm trunk is not “soft” in the crash sense; it is still a substantial fixed object, with some test/report evidence suggesting it can snap at higher loads but is not comparable to a crushable barrier[5][7][13].

## Will the car spin or yaw around the tree?
- Yes, off-centre impacts commonly induce yaw/rotation because the force acts at the corner rather than through the vehicle’s center of mass[1][4][8].
- Crash reconstruction references explicitly note that if a vehicle hits a pole off-centre, it will usually rotate away from the collision[1].
- Tree crash case material also describes post-impact yaw, including a vehicle yawing about 90 degrees after striking a tree[4].
- The exact path depends on speed, tire grip, bumper/rail engagement, and where the trunk contacts the car, but a corner strike at 30–60 km/h often produces a deflection, spin, or partial wrap-around motion rather than a straight stop[1][4][8].

## Practical bottom line
- At 30–60 km/h, a front-corner hit on a palm tree is typically a **severe fixed-object crash**, not a “glancing safe miss”[2][13][14].
- The car will usually **yaw/rotate away from the tree** and may come to rest at an angle or after a spin[1][4][8].
- The palm tree may **remain standing, crack, or snap** depending on its size and root/soil conditions, but roadside sources still treat palms as dangerous rigid hazards rather than forgiving objects[5][13][15].
