In many pursuit policies, a fleeing car is treated as effectively **stopped/caught** once it is boxed in and slowed to a stop, or when it is at or near a complete stop and officers have taken control of the vehicle’s movement[2][4][6][10]. A car creeping at 2–5 km/h is usually **not yet treated as fully stopped** if the policy defines boxing-in as slowing the target vehicle to a stop, though some policies allow boxing-in of a vehicle already “at or near a complete stop” to prevent renewed flight[2][4][6].

## What policies say
- Connecticut’s model policy says boxing-in is done at **relatively low speeds**, and after the violator is blocked, police units **slowly and gradually reduce speed, causing the violator to stop**[2].
- A California agency policy defines boxing-in as maneuvering into the path of a vehicle traveling **slower than 20 mph**, or a stopped vehicle, with the intent of slowing and stopping it or keeping it stopped[4].
- San Antonio’s policy defines boxing-in as positioning vehicles in front of and behind a suspect vehicle when it is **at or near a complete stop** to prevent flight[6].
- New Jersey’s policy says boxing-in/heading-off is allowed only under extraordinary circumstances, **at low speeds**, with supervisor approval or an imminent threat[7][13][15].

## Typical speed/end-condition ranges
- **Boxing-in / rolling roadblock:** generally **low-speed only**; one policy explicitly uses **below 20 mph** as the cutoff for boxing-in, and another says it is done when the target is **at or near a stop**[4][6].
- **PIT/TVI:** one model policy says PIT/TVI is most effective between about **25 and 45 mph**, and “anything less than 25 mph won’t work”[1].
- **End condition:** the maneuver is considered successful when the suspect vehicle is **forced to stop**, and police then secure the vehicle and occupants[2][4][6][10].

## Practical answer to your example
If a suspect car is boxed in and only creeping at **2–5 km/h**, most policies would treat that as **effectively stopped or being brought to a stop**, not as an active high-speed pursuit, because the purpose of boxing-in is to reduce the vehicle to a stop and keep it stopped[2][4][6][10]. But whether officers label it “stopped” versus “still moving” can matter operationally; policies often still require caution until the vehicle is fully immobilized and the occupants are secured[4][6][7].

## Important nuance
- “**Boxed in**” does not always mean “**fully neutralized**”; many policies still expect officers to maintain control until the vehicle is fully stopped and there is no realistic escape route[2][4][6].
- PIT is a **different tactic** from boxing-in, with a much higher typical speed window and different termination conditions[1].
