There is no reliable, universal speed threshold at which a struck car’s engine will “keep running” versus “stop/get destroyed” in a T-bone crash. Whether the engine keeps running depends much more on where the intrusion occurs, whether the battery/fuel pump/wiring are damaged, and whether a fuel-cutoff device is triggered than on impact speed alone.[1][13]

In standard side-impact crash tests, the struck vehicle is hit at 50 km/h by a deformable barrier at 90 degrees, and the test protocol records fuel-system integrity, but it does not treat engine shutdown as a simple speed-based outcome.[1][2][3] In other words, a car can be heavily damaged at 50 km/h and still have an intact powertrain, or it can stall immediately if fuel delivery or electrical systems are interrupted.[1][13]

For the struck car, “engine still running” is plausible in moderate side impacts where the hit is localized away from the powertrain and fuel/electrical systems; “engine stops” is more likely when the impact crushes the engine bay, severs electrical power, or trips the inertia/fuel cutoff switch.[1][13] A fuel inertia switch is specifically designed to shut off the fuel supply after a heavy impact, and its documentation states that when it operates, the engine should stop.[13]

For the striking car in a frontal hit into another car’s side, engine survival is generally more likely at lower speeds and less likely as the impact speed rises, but again there is no clean threshold from the crash-test data you asked for.[1][3] At 30 km/h, many modern vehicles would be expected to keep the engine and drivetrain intact, though front-end damage may be substantial; at 50 km/h, significant front-structure damage becomes much more likely, but the engine can still survive if the crash pulse is managed and intrusion is limited; at 80 km/h, catastrophic front-end deformation is much more likely and engine survival becomes much less likely.[1][3] That said, these are engineering expectations, not a guaranteed cutoff, because the IIHS 50 km/h side-impact protocol is about occupant protection and fuel-system integrity, not a direct engine-destruction threshold.[1][3]

So the practical answer is: there is no single side-impact speed at which an engine “typically” survives or dies; 50 km/h is a standard severe test speed for side impacts, and engine shutoff is usually a consequence of damage to fuel/electrical systems or activation of the inertia cutoff, not speed alone.[1][13]
